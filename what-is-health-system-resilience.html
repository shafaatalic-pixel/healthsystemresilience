Definition · The idea behind HSREP

What is health-system resilience as economic protection?

It is the idea that a health system's ability to keep working through a shock is also what keeps households, employers and public budgets from breaking. When care holds through an outbreak, a disaster or a funding cut, people keep their savings, their jobs and their trust. When it fails, the cost does not disappear: it moves onto families first, then onto clinics and the public purse. HSREP treats the two as one investment seen from two sides.

The definition

What do the two terms mean?

Public health talks about resilience. Economics talks about financial protection. HSREP's argument is that they describe the same thing from two sides.

Health-system resilience

Can the system keep working?

The World Health Organization's Eastern Mediterranean office describes it as the ability of a health system exposed to a shock "to resist, absorb, accommodate and recover" from its effects, keeping its essential structures and functions.1

Economic protection

Can people get care without going broke?

Usually called financial protection: people get the care they need without the cost pushing them into hardship or poverty. It is one of the two things universal health coverage is measured by, under Sustainable Development Goal indicator 3.8.2.2

HSREP's claim

One investment, two returns

Health protection is economic infrastructure, not a budget line to cut. A system that holds under shock is what protects household income, the workforce and public finances when the shock arrives.

How the cost travels

Who pays when a health system fails?

Households pay first, then clinics and public budgets. The question is never whether a shock costs money. It is who absorbs the cost, and how early.

StepWhen the system holdsWhen it does not
1. The shockAn illness, a bill, an outbreak or a disaster arrives.The same shock arrives.
2. The householdCare is reachable and affordable; work and schooling continue.Families delay care, borrow, sell assets or pay out of pocket.
3. The clinic and the public purseServices absorb the demand and keep running.Clinics, budgets and public services take the overflow, often as emergency care.
4. The economyLivelihoods, productivity and trust are protected.Lost income, lost workdays and lost trust outlast the shock.

This chain is HSREP's framing of the argument made across its Season 1 articles. It is a model for reading the evidence below, not a measured result.

Two figures for Bangladesh, and why

HSREP's Season 1 articles cite "nearly 69 percent" (68.5% in the accounts), from the Bangladesh National Health Accounts 1997 to 2020, which measures out-of-pocket payments as a share of total health expenditure in 2020. The 79.3% above is the international series for 2023, measured as a share of current health expenditure. The two use different years and different denominators; both say that households carry most of the cost.

Why two countries

The United States and Bangladesh finance health in very different ways. HSREP works across both because the same failure shows up in each: when the system does not hold, the household pays.

Limits

What does it not mean?

Not "spend more, anywhere"

The argument is about where protection fails: prevention that is recommended but not completed, first contact people cannot afford, a workforce that does not stay. A new building does not protect a household that cannot afford the follow-up.

Not only for disasters

Earthquakes and outbreaks make the failure visible. Much of the cost arrives quietly, one bill and one skipped screening at a time.

Not partisan, and not proof

HSREP is independent and non-partisan. This page states a position and the evidence behind it; it does not claim that resilience spending has been shown to pay for itself in every setting.

Common questions

Quick answers.

What does "health-system resilience as economic protection" mean?

It means a health system that keeps working through a shock also protects household income, the workforce and public budgets. HSREP treats health protection as economic infrastructure rather than a budget line to cut.

Is resilience the same as emergency preparedness?

Preparedness is part of it. Resilience also covers the everyday shocks: a bill a family cannot pay, a screening that is recommended but never completed, a clinic that loses its staff.

Is this only a problem in low-income countries?

No. In the United States, 41% of adults have debt from medical or dental bills (KFF, 2022). In Bangladesh, households paid 79.3% of current health expenditure out of pocket in 2023 (World Bank). The financing differs; in both, households carry costs the system did not absorb.

How is economic protection measured?

Under Sustainable Development Goal indicator 3.8.2, by the share of people whose out-of-pocket health spending exceeds 40% of their household's discretionary budget. By that measure, an estimated 2.1 billion people faced financial hardship in 2022, according to WHO and the World Bank. Reports before 2025 used thresholds of 10% and 25% of the total household budget.
