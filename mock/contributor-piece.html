In Brief5 min read
  • Campus health services recommend more preventive care than students complete: [figure, source] of recommended screenings and vaccinations at [institution or national sample] are never finished. The recommendation is the easy part.
  • The gap is not knowledge. Students know what they were told to do. It is friction: a second appointment, a bill they cannot read, a clinic that closes when classes end.
  • A campus health office is the one place that can see the whole path, from the recommendation to the completed visit, and redesign the path rather than repeat the message.
  • A screening completed at 21 is protection bought early: cheaper than the illness it prevents, and cheaper than the semester it would cost.

Every autumn, a campus health office tells a few thousand students the same thing: come back. Come back for the second dose, the follow-up, the screening your family history says you should not skip. Most of them mean to. [Figure, source] of them never do. The office logs the recommendation, the student leaves with a leaflet, and the appointment that would have cost forty minutes becomes, years later, a diagnosis that costs a great deal more.

This is the Prevention Adoption Gap in miniature. HSREP describes it at the scale of a health system: a screening is recommended, a screening is not completed, and the difference is paid for later by the household and by everyone else. On a campus the same gap has a shorter path and a smaller budget, which is exactly why it is worth studying there. Everything that goes wrong between a recommendation and a completed visit happens inside one building, under one office, to a population that comes back every semester.

Where the path breaks

Ask students why they did not return and the answers are not about belief. [Survey or interview finding, source]. The second appointment falls in exam week. The reminder went to a university inbox nobody reads. The bill arrived with a code and no explanation, and the safest thing to do with a bill you do not understand is nothing. None of these is a failure of health education. Each is a small design decision that assumes the student will carry the rest of the work.

A health promotion office is unusual in that it can see all of these decisions at once. It knows when the reminder went out and where. It knows which clinics close at five. It can find out what the bill looked like. In a large health system these facts sit in four departments; on a campus they sit in one corridor.

Redesign the path, not the message

The interventions that close this kind of gap elsewhere are not new. [Evidence: default scheduling, mailed at-home testing, navigation, source]. What they share is that they move work from the student to the system: the follow-up is booked before the student leaves the room, the reminder arrives on the phone they actually use, the cost is stated in a sentence before the visit rather than in a code after it.

The economic case is short. [Cost of the completed screening vs. cost of the condition it prevents, source]. Against that, the cost of a text message and a default appointment is close to nothing. A screening completed at twenty-one is protection bought early, and a campus is one of the few places where the purchase can be made for a whole cohort at once.

What I would ask of a health promotion office is therefore small: pick one recommendation, count how many students complete it, change one step of the path, and count again. That is a semester’s work. It is also, in miniature, the pilot HSREP is proposing for a safety-net clinic, and if it works on a campus it is evidence that the larger version can work too.

About contributor pieces
What is a contributor piece?

A short argument (about 1,000 to 1,400 words) written against a declared Season lens by a graduate contributor at a contributing institution, reviewed first at that institution and then edited by HSREP. It is published with the author’s name, affiliation and ORCID, a DOI, a media package and a roundtable question the author moderates.

How does review work?

Two stages. The institution’s first reviewer reads for substance and evidence. HSREP’s editor then edits for accuracy, clarity, length and house style; the author sees the edited version and has three working days to flag a factual error before it publishes.

Who owns it, and can it be reused?

The author keeps the copyright. HSREP holds exclusive first publication for 90 days; after that the author may republish anywhere with a line crediting HSREP. The author may use the piece in coursework, a thesis or a portfolio at any time.
