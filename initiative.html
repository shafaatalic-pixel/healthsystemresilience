Proposed · in development · no site, partner or funding yet The Prevention Adoption Gap

From a recommended test to a finished one.

Health centers already know what gets people screened for colorectal cancer. What fails is follow-through: kits that never come back, and positive results that never reach a colonoscopy. The Prevention Adoption Initiative proposes a weekly routine, run by a clinic’s own staff, that closes those gaps.

Your staff make the calls, mail the kits and navigate patients.HSREP works beside them: finds out why patients stall, writes the scripts, trains the team, keeps the list.An independent evaluator judges whether it worked.

01 · The gap

Two problems, measured on two different groups.

Only about 8% of U.S. adults 35 and older receive all fifteen high-priority preventive services (Borsky et al., Health Affairs, 2018). Colorectal cancer screening is the part this pilot takes on. Screening completion counts everyone who is due. Follow-up counts only people whose test came back positive. They are not steps of one funnel, so this page never multiplies them.

A · Up to date on colorectal screening
Share of the eligible population
U.S. adults, 2023 (ages 50–75)63.5%
Michigan health centers, CY2025 UDS (45–75)48.74%
Federally qualified health centers, national, CY2025 UDS (45–75)44.89%
Healthy People 2030 target: 72.8% (objective C-07, ages 50–75)

Age ranges differ: health-center data (HRSA UDS) has covered ages 45–75 since 2023. Close measures, not identical ones.

B · Colonoscopy after a positive stool test
Share of people who screen positive
Reported range, U.S. settings24–75%

Shown as a range because studies disagree. NCQA has proposed a measure for it: colonoscopy within 180 days of a positive test (COF-E, HEDIS MY2027).

Why follow-through, not a new method

In the largest real-world health-center trial (STOP CRC, 26 clinics), the same mailed-kit program moved completion anywhere from −7 to +18 points, +3.4 on average. The main difference was how well each clinic carried it out.

Sources: Borsky et al., Health Affairs 2018; Healthy People 2030 C-07; HRSA UDS CY2025 national and Michigan indicators, accessed 3 August 2026; Coronado et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2018. More in Evidence and questions.

02 · How it works

One routine, every week.

Every method in it is already recommended by the Community Preventive Services Task Force. The routine makes sure each step happens for every patient who is due.

What happens

Everyone overdue for a stool test, and every positive test still waiting for a colonoscopy, pulled from the nightly file.

Sources: CDC Community Preventive Services Task Force, colorectal cancer screening findings, accessed 3 August 2026; Borsky et al., Health Affairs 2018; Healthy People 2030 objective C-07 (2023 baseline 63.5%, target 72.8%); HRSA Uniform Data System CY2025 national and Michigan performance indicators, accessed 3 August 2026. How patient data would be handled

The host site remains the covered entity and keeps the key that links codes to patients. HSREP receives only the coded nightly file, under a data use agreement set up through the academic partner’s IRB. Phase 1 is designed so that no business associate agreement is needed, because HSREP never handles names, record numbers or full dates. A BAA would come in only if a later phase hosts patient data.

Consent and outreach run under the site’s own policies. Operational materials will be reviewed by counsel before any launch.

What is the Prevention Adoption Gap?
The distance between preventive care that is recommended and reimbursed and the care that is actually completed. The failure is adoption and execution, not the underlying science: people start a preventive pathway far more often than they finish it.
Why track follow-up colonoscopy, not just the screening test?
Because that is where the pathway breaks and where lives are lost. A positive stool test is only a warning; the diagnosis needs a follow-up colonoscopy, and nationally, between 24% and 75% of positive tests get one. In Italian screening-program data, people who never completed one were about twice as likely to die of colorectal cancer (hazard ratio 2.03; Zorzi, Gut 2022), and in a 70,124-patient Kaiser Permanente cohort the odds of advanced-stage disease beyond twelve months were more than three times those of a colonoscopy done within a month (Corley, JAMA 2017). Both are associations rather than measured causal effects. Most registries mark screening “done” at the stool test; this initiative treats an unresolved positive result as an open, time-critical item, aligned with COF-E, the follow-up measure NCQA has proposed for HEDIS MY2027 (colonoscopy within 180 days of a positive test). The dashboard demo shows that queue.
Is the colorectal-cancer screening pilot running yet?
No. The pilot is proposed and in development. Nothing has been deployed, and all operational designs shown here are illustrative.
Where would the first pilot run?
The proposed first site is a safety-net setting in Southeast Michigan, chosen because that is where the completion gap is widest and the toolkit can be tested under real constraints.
Is the initiative specific to colorectal cancer?
No. It is a disease-agnostic toolkit for closing preventive-care completion gaps. Colorectal-cancer screening completion is simply the first demonstration.
How can an organization host or partner on the pilot?
Organizations that own this problem (health systems, safety-net clinics, payers, or public-health agencies) can start a conversation through the platform to host the pilot, partner on it, or fund it.
Does the first pilot use AI?
No. The first site runs the completion loop on transparent rules: days overdue, days since a positive test, prior no-shows. Reminders and navigation are delivered by the site’s own staff through its existing channels. A predictive, explainable model and conversational outreach are on the roadmap, to be considered only after a validated pilot, under the site’s governance and a BAA. No patient messaging is automated in the first pilot.
How is this different from CDC's Colorectal Cancer Control Program?
CRCCP funds the same evidence-based levers, and this initiative is designed to complement it, not compete with it. The difference is where results actually leak: STOP CRC showed that results depend on how well each step is carried out, and no federal program tracks that. This initiative adds a weekly dashboard that tracks whether each step happened, treats follow-up after a positive test (the proposed COF-E measure) as the core object rather than an afterthought, and reuses the same engine across diseases. A CRCCP-funded site could run this loop inside its existing award.
What is the Prevention Adoption Initiative?
It is a proposed, disease-agnostic toolkit that closes the gap between recommended and completed preventive care in safety-net settings, beginning with a colorectal-cancer screening pilot. It is in development; nothing has been deployed.
Limitations, always visible

This is an HSREP initiative in development. Nothing is deployed and no patient data has been collected.

One site measured before and after cannot rule out changes that would have happened anyway. To make the result believable, the pilot should publish its target in advance (for example, at least 10 points above the site’s own trend and above the ~3.4-point STOP CRC average), add a comparison clinic where possible, name who pays for staff time after any grant ends, and make sure every positive test gets its follow-up.

The first three months show whether the routine can run, not whether screening rates rose.

Published effect sizes are not the pilot’s expected results.
